### Per-class rates, which are what transfer

| | value | 95% Wilson | cluster resampling range |
|---|---:|---|---|
| **Recall / TPR** | **0.733** | 0.556 – 0.858 | **0.600 – 0.900** |
| **False-positive rate** | **0.455** | 0.340 – 0.574 | **0.407 – 0.493** |
| Precision | 0.423 | 0.299 – 0.558 | — |

**The right-hand column is NOT a 95% confidence interval, and naming it
one would be an overclaim.** Bootstrap coverage assumes clusters drawn from
a population. These are intent sentences chosen by the author to span a
design grid -- a deliberate selection, not a sample of merchant intents. It
says how far the estimate moves when those particular sentences are
resampled, and nothing about long-run coverage.

**Quote the cluster bootstrap.** Wilson was pre-registered and is kept for
that reason, but it assumes 96 independent observations and only **5
distinct intent sentences** contributed a scored row. Rows sharing a
sentence share whatever the raters decided it means, so those rows are not
independent and Wilson is the wrong instrument for them.

The bootstrap resamples whole sentence groups. **It will not always be the
wider of the two.** Resampling 5 things is coarse: the percentiles move in
visible steps and one metric can come out narrower than Wilson purely as an
artifact of how few groups there are. That is not evidence Wilson was right
on that metric — it is the same small-cluster problem showing up with the
opposite sign, and neither interval should be read to three decimal places.

A cluster whose rows were all excluded — as `unlabelable`, or for want of a
majority — drops out entirely, which lowers the count above and coarsens the
bootstrap further. See `PROTOCOL-armE-AMENDMENT-1.md`.

**Precision is base-rate dependent and this corpus's balance is a design
choice.** Quote recall and the false-positive rate. The intervals are wide
because 96 scored rows cannot support three decimal places, and reporting
the point estimates alone would be the overclaim this arm exists to avoid.

### What the exclusion costs

The guard **refused 14** of these and **allowed 10**. Dropping them is not
a neutral act: they are exactly the rows a careful reader can argue about.

If the permissive reading is taken instead — every contested row counted
`in-intent`, so each refusal among them becomes a false positive:

| | precision | FPR |
|---|---:|---:|
| **as published** (24 excluded) | 0.423 | 0.455 |
| **permissive reading** (24 counted `in-intent`) | **0.333** | **0.489** |

Recall is unchanged either way: the reading moves rows into the negative
class only, so no true positive or false negative is affected.

**Neither column is the answer.** The published figure excludes rows with
no ground truth, which is the pre-registered rule; the second shows what
the other reading would cost. Precision is the metric that moves, which is
a further reason to quote recall and the false-positive rate.

## What this does not establish

- **Not a rate of agent misbehaviour.** The requests are constructed.
  Arm C asked how often an agent would make one, failed, and that failure
  stands.
- **Not merchant traffic**, and not a fraud rate.
- Precision is a property of this corpus's class balance.
- The false-positive rate is a property of the intent-to-authorization
  compilation as much as of the guard.
- One author designed the dimensions. Enumeration removes selection bias
  within the grid, not the grid's blind spots.
- Raters are independent of the implementation, not of the author who
  recruited them.
